OFFICIAL COMMUNICATIONS 
 
President Osili recognized the Honorable Mayor Joseph H. Hogsett , who made the following 
remarks: 
 
President Osili, Vice President Adamson, Leaders Lewis and Mowery, members of this distinguished council: 
 
Seven years ago, we were preparing the first City-County budget since I had taken office. Here were the facts:  Due to 
property tax caps, the City was facing a 50-million-dollar structural deficit; DPW equipment was often older than the 
hardworking AFSCME crew members who operated it; and as a years-long hiring freeze for new IMPD officers came to an 
end, first-year IMPD recruits were being paid just 39,000 dollars a year.  
 
Now, let’s look at today…the structural deficit is over five years gone, and rating agencies have consistently upgraded the 
City’s credit; together, we have passed balanced budgets since 2018, often with unanimous – and always bi-partisan – 
support; we have increased first-year pay for IMPD officers to 72,000 dollars a year. That’s an 85% increase since 2016; by 
the end of 2023, we will have hired nearly 800 police officers since 2016. That includes an extra recruit class this year to 
bring even more officers into the department; we are making a multi-year investment of over a billion dollars into roads, 
sidewalks, and other infrastructure without a tax increase; the unemployment rate remains at historic lows, and Downtown 
alone is slated for 9.5 billion dollars in development over the next several years.  It’s clear: Indianapolis is on a roll.  
 
Tonight’s proposal aims to capture that progress and accelerate the city’s growth. It would provide 1.5 billion dollars to 
improve public safety, enhance infrastructure, and lift quality of life throughout the neighborhoods of Indianapolis – all without 
tax increases or selling off public assets.  I do think it is important to mention that this will be the first proposal since 2019 to 
be made without significant aid from federal pandemic recovery programs. Some cities will struggle this year due to that 
absence. Indianapolis will not. Thanks to prudent fiscal planning, we never once had to rely on federal money to cover basic 
services. Today, that financial advantage permits us to expand our city’s ambition, and address Council priorities in every 
single Council district. While individual City-County departments will present a fuller account in the coming weeks, tonight I 
will outline some of the most significant pieces of the 2024 fiscal plan. Let me begin where I often do – the importance of 
public safety.

In the fall of 2021, this Council approved a 3-year, 150-million-dollar public safety strategy to address pandemic-related 
increases in crime. It had three prongs: law enforcement, community-based anti-violence efforts, and the root causes of 
crime. Since we implemented that strategy, we have experienced declines in nearly every major crime category. That 
includes non-fatal shootings and robberies. Last year, we saw a 16% decline in murders, with a further 12% decline so far 
this year. In no way are we declaring victory. Downward trends aren’t the same as meeting a goal. I also want to make clear 
that these downward trends preceded, and continue, despite the passage of permitless carry. However, since that state law 
went into effect, we have seen a spike in accidental shootings, youth violence, and non-criminal homicides. But we aren’t 
sitting idly by. In May, I announced an acceleration of our violence reduction plan. It is reflected in this budget.  
The portions dealing with state preemption of local gun law have been widely reported. But I want to get into what we will 
change independent of the state legislature.  
 
First, the law enforcement piece: we are budgeting 323 million dollars for the Indianapolis Metropolitan Police Department, 
the largest total in history. We are increasing first-year IMPD recruit wages to 72,000 dollars. As I said, that is an 85% 
increase from 2016.  The 2024 budget also contains a 3% overall raise for veteran IMPD officers, helping ensure we retain 
those who choose to serve. And they’re not alone. This 2024 budget proposal includes raises for emergency responders 
across the enterprise, including Marion County Sheriff’s Office deputies, and members of the Indianapolis Fire Department. 
Both departments will also receive retention bonuses at the end of this calendar year. And it includes even more funding for 
IFD equipment. Over the course of 8 years, we will have completed a full replacement of their frontline apparatus while 
substantially improving the quality of their reserve fleet. We have also included 1.5 million dollars for new public safety 
technology, like dashcams, license plate readers, and public safety cameras – effective technology that also increases the 
transparency of law enforcement. This budget also funds our more innovative programs into the next year. For example, our 
Special Assistant United States Attorney partnership will allow us to hold the worst of the worst gun violence offenders here 
in Marion County to account at the federal level.  
 
But ‘law enforcement’ is only one-third of our anti-violence plan. Just as important were investments into community groups, 
and addressing root causes – especially mental illness. Last month, our Downtown district saw the debut of the Clinician-Led 
Community Response team. These are healthcare technicians dispatched to 911 calls dealing with mental health. They 
divert illness into care and treatment – not incarceration. Members of that team join us tonight. Would you all please stand 
and be recognized? This budget will fund that team’s expansion into the East district, bringing us closer to a fully staffed, 
24/7, clinician-led response for some of our highest-volume call areas. Tonight’s budget proposal also doubles the capacity 
of our Assessment and Intervention Center – the cornerstone of our criminal justice reform effort. With that increase, the AIC 
will build on the 4,600 people they have served so far – outside the criminal justice system – since opening in 2020. And as 
we transition away from federal recovery dollars, I am proud that this budget permanently secures annual funding of the 
Peacemaker program. Whether connecting people to resources or stopping conflicts, Peacemakers have played a significant 
role in the declines we’ve seen in violence and retaliatory crimes. I know we have several in the crowd tonight. Would you 
please stand and be recognized? 
  
Before leaving public safety, I want to note that this budget also continues some of our most effective programs focused on 
young people.  Indy Achieves has helped nearly four thousand Marion County students access higher education since its 
inception. And, Project Indy has connected thousands of teens with jobs that provide foundational skills for a later career.  
These programs are transforming the lives of Indianapolis residents, and repaying taxpayers by showing that our best long-
term crime-prevention strategy is to invest in our future.  
 
Are you ready for more critical investments in the future? Good, because next we’ll turn to infrastructure.  Last year’s 
proposal outlined a billion dollars over five years for roads, bridges, sidewalks, trails, bike lanes, drainage, and more.  At the 
time, there were some who informed us that we should stop talking about the state road funding formula, because the 
Indiana General Assembly was never going to help Indianapolis. Well, I have an update: the nay-sayers were wrong. This 
year, in partnership with state Senator Aaron Freeman, and others, we secured a long-overdue correction based on our 
city’s population. The result will be millions more for infrastructure throughout Indianapolis’s neighborhoods.  Consequently, 
we are able to present to the Council tonight an increased five-year capital plan of nearly 1.2 billion dollars.  - That includes 
an added 25 million dollars for residential streets, now totaling more than 100 million dollars since 2021. Through that 
funding, we have secured full rehabs for stretches of roadway that have been overlooked for decades.  The overall 
infrastructure plan also includes measures to improve pedestrian safety and support projects that calm historically dangerous 
streets.  Best of all, we can muster this 1.2 billion dollars without a single tax increase on Marion County residents – who, by 
the way, already pay more than their fair share for roads. But we’re a long way from ‘fair’ when it comes to Central Indiana’s 
infrastructure. So, along with many other Hoosier cities, we are continuing to advocate for a more equitable approach to 
state-level road funding.  
 
Speaking of state legislative achievements, I want to talk about another key win reflected in this budget. Earlier this year, the 
General Assembly passed Senate Bill 46, authored by state Senator Jack Sandlin, in bipartisan fashion. The law gives us an 
additional tool to keep longtime residents in their homes by limiting the explosive growth in property tax assessments 
experienced by far too many. Tonight, I’m proud to announce a partnership with the City-County Council and the Marion 
County Commissioners for a pilot of this anti-displacement tool in the Riverside neighborhood. I am confident this program 
will prove successful and expand to other parts of the city in the future. In January, park-lovers like myself got some welcome 
news when the Lilly Endowment announced their 80-million-dollar gift to the Indianapolis Parks Department. It was a 
wonderful affirmation of our focus on investment for many of our city’s historically underfunded parks. Folks can go to 
INDY.GOV to view how that money will be spent at 42 different locations.  Our 2024 budget proposal supports that generous 
gift with an increase in funding for parks maintenance – because those new additions won’t be new forever. Also included 
are park safety measures, like new public safety cameras at nine locations.  Taken with the Lilly Endowment’s gift, and this 
administration’s Circle City Forward initiative, these efforts represent one of the largest investments into Indy parks in years.  
 
Tonight I am also excited to unveil new investments in a city department that too often goes unheralded: the Department of 
Business and Neighborhood Services. They have been a critical part of new development across the city.  Since 2016 BNS

has issued development permits at more than twice the annual volume of when I took office.  Added to that, standard 
turnaround times for these permits are less than three weeks – a fraction of the 4 to 6 months in peer cities like Nashville or 
Austin.  This proposal also supports BNS with 2 million dollars for repairs to neighborhood alleys, a long-overdue investment.  
In fact, the last several years have seen many long-overdue City obligations finally fulfilled.  
 
For that, I turn to added investments concerning diversity and equity in our City.  In 2019, the Office of Minority and Women 
Business Development embarked on a disparity study of City contracts. At that point, the City had failed to administer this 
five-year assessment in more than two decades.  Tonight’s budget would restore the five-year cycle to ensure we continue 
meeting this important obligation.  And to that point, this budget would also create an Office of Equity, Belonging and 
Inclusion. It is past time that we join our peer cities by putting an overall strategic vision to our enterprise and community DEI 
efforts. The new office will administer staff training, collect relevant data, and lead community programs to address the needs 
of our large and diverse community.   
 
I have said this for several years now, but it remains true: we are living through one of the most consequential eras in city 
history. How we lead, how we invest, how we plan in this time will determine our city’s future to a large degree. And despite 
our challenges, we must be bold – because we are also living at a time of tremendous opportunity. As I said at the start, 
Indianapolis is in a position of fiscal strength relative to many of our peer cities. So we can afford to put 1.2 billion dollars into 
infrastructure while also negotiating a better funding deal from the Statehouse; so we can fully catalyze the Lilly 
Endowment’s 80-million-dollar grant for parks; and so we can facilitate major Downtown projects like the expansion of the 
Indiana Convention Center and the construction of the new Signia Hotel, helping to attract 9.5 billion dollars of Downtown 
development.  
 
Colleagues, I look forward to the weeks ahead, and I am hopeful for the passage of yet another balanced, principled, fully-
funded, and bold City-County operating budget.  
 
I thank you

SPECIAL ORDERS - PUBLIC HEARING 
 
President Osili called on General Counsel LeAnnette Pierce to review the ground rules for public 
testimony as adopted by this body.  Ms. Pierce reminded Council members and the public of the 
ground rules for the public comment portion of the agenda.  She said that in order for everyone to 
have a fair chance to speak and be heard, it is important to observe the following rules.  First, each 
speaker will be limited  to two minutes.  Second, any public comments must reasonably relate to 
the agenda item under consideration.  Third, speakers who stray from the item under consideration 
or become unduly repetitious may be asked to move on to their next point or conclude t heir 
comments.  Finally, attendees who cause disruptions that prevent the Council from proceeding 
through today’s agenda in a reasonably efficient manner will be removed.  Ms. Pierce added that 
some types of threatening speech or incitement to violence are not protected by the First 
Amendment and will be dealt with if they come up.  President Osili called for consent to adopt the 
ground rules for public testimony, and consent was given.
